# How I got here

A living account of the places, people and opportunities that shaped the work I do.

From Ghana to China and Ireland: the people, opportunities and questions that shaped Elvis Kwame Ofori’s work and writing.

![](../assets/profile.jpg)

Elvis Kwame Ofori standing outdoors in a patterned cardigan.

I studied accounting in Ghana, and I now build agricultural and land-use models in Ireland. Neither the start nor the middle was planned. This page is the longer version of how one led to the other, and an attempt to give some credit to the people and places that made the route possible. The [About page](../about.llms.md) has the short version.

Looking back makes the path look neater than it was. I studied accounting in Accra, did National Service with the National Petroleum Authority and was posted to Takoradi. A scholarship took me to northern China, where research pulled me towards policy, sustainability and energy. Another turn brought me to Ireland, to agriculture and land use, and to the farmers who live with decisions made a long way from their land.

I have never read the opportunities I received as proof that I was the most exceptional person in the room. There were people more brilliant than me. What I did learn was to be ready when a door opened, to make hay, as the old saying goes, while the sun shines. And very little of it happened alone. Teachers, family, friends, colleagues and supervisors opened doors, gave advice, trusted me with responsibility, or simply made an unfamiliar place feel less strange. This page is partly my attempt to remember them, and I expect to keep adding to it.

## Ghana: where it began

I studied **Accounting at the University of Professional Studies, Accra**. It looks some distance from the work I do now, but I do not think of it as an abandoned beginning. Accounting taught me to pay attention to numbers and organisations, and to the difference between what something appears to be and what the underlying records actually show. It took me years to see how useful that instinct would become.

After university I did my National Service with Ghana’s **National Petroleum Authority**. I had been living in Accra, and the posting sent me to Takoradi, one of the first times life moved me somewhere I had not planned to go. Takoradi was also my first taste of real responsibility. I worked nights and double shifts, and I was trusted to supervise. It was not glamorous, but it was an education of its own. University had taught me concepts; work began to show me what happens when procedures, rules and decisions meet the people who have to carry them out, and how differently one organisation looks depending on where inside it you stand.

I would not have called any of that an interest in policy implementation at the time. But the question stayed with me, and a few years later it came back as research: one of my early papers, published in [*Resources Policy*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resourpol.2023.103368), looked at how to measure safety performance in Ghana’s oil and gas industry.

## China: learning to think in frameworks

In 2019 I received a **full scholarship for an MSc in Management Science and Engineering** and moved to **Taiyuan, in northern China**. I knew China would be different. I was less prepared for how different the north would feel to someone arriving from Ghana, and the cold introduced itself quickly.

The larger change was intellectual. China was where I learned to approach problems through frameworks. Instead of seeing only a result, I started asking what structure produced it. Who were the actors? What assumptions sat underneath the analysis, and what evidence was it using? What happened when a policy moved off the page and into an economy or a community? My research ranged across sustainability, development, environmental policy and energy economics. Energy held my attention because its decisions were so large: governments, industries and major firms could change what happened across a whole system, and thinking at that scale taught me to look for the links between institutions, incentives and outcomes. I later began doctoral study in China as well.

![Elvis Kwame Ofori standing outdoors with a group of friends and fellow students during his time in China](images/china-community.jpg)

With people who were part of my life in China.

*China became much more than a scholarship or a degree. The people around me became part of the education too.*

Part of my life there never appeared on a transcript. I taught **English as a second language to Chinese students**, and I especially enjoyed the younger learners. I still love watching someone grow more confident as something that looked difficult begins to make sense. The same instinct carried into my work with colleagues. When someone was struggling with an analysis, I liked sitting down with them and working through it: not which button to press, but what the numbers were saying and whether the interpretation held up. I have come to think of data analysis almost as a conversation. You learn how to talk to data. You also have to learn when to stop talking long enough to let the data answer back.

That pleasure in teaching and explaining has followed me into almost everything I have done since.

## Ireland: bringing the question closer to the ground

Eventually I left the doctoral path I had begun in China and moved to the **University of Galway** for a different PhD. Ireland moved my research sideways in a way I had not fully expected. Much of my earlier work was in energy economics, where the important actors were governments, industries and large institutions. Agriculture and land use brought me much closer to individual choices.

A national government can set a climate target, a model can describe a pathway, and a sector can be told that its emissions must fall. But eventually somebody owns the land. Somebody has animals to feed, bills to pay, machinery already bought, a family involved in the farm, or a good reason for continuing to do something that looks inefficient from above. That shift pulled my attention towards farmers, land-use decisions, behavioural responses and the way costs are distributed across people and places. Later, while continuing the PhD, I joined the wider **FORESIGHT and FUSION** research environment at Galway as a **Research Assistant**, which drew me further into agricultural, climate and land-use modelling.

![Elvis Kwame Ofori standing with other participants at the Agriculture and Land Use in Ireland conference](images/agriculture-land-use-conference.jpg)

At the Agriculture & Land Use in Ireland conference.

*Research eventually gave me a community as well as a subject.*

The subject had changed a great deal since accounting and my energy work, but the underlying questions had changed less than I first thought. Who makes the decision, and with what information? Which constraints are invisible from a distance? Who benefits, and who pays? What happens between a policy being designed and somebody actually having to respond to it? My doctoral work sits inside those questions. A national model may say that livestock numbers should fall, that land should change use or that a technology should be adopted. A farmer still has to decide what to do on Monday morning. The distance between the aggregate pathway and that decision has become the part of the work I care about most.

## The people along the way

Since leaving Ghana in 2019 I have sometimes felt like a wanderer. Countries change, and so do the rooms and the people around the table. You learn new routes to work, new food, new weather, new ways of speaking, and all the small rules nobody explains because everyone else already knows them. Then, slowly, somewhere unfamiliar starts to feel ordinary.

The institutions are easy to list. The people are not. There have been classmates who became friends, colleagues who patiently explained things I probably should have known, supervisors who trusted me, people who shared meals, people who helped when I was new somewhere, and people whose paths crossed mine briefly and still left something behind. That may be the part of moving countries a CV hides best. A CV records the year you arrived. It does not record who helped make the place feel less strange.

## Beyond the desk

Research has also given me reasons to travel. One trip took me to Washington, D.C., for the **World Bank LAND Conference**. The formal sessions and presentations were the reason for going, but a conference is rarely only its programme. There are the conversations afterwards, the walk through an unfamiliar city, and the things you notice that send your thinking somewhere the programme never intended. I have come to value both halves.

![Elvis Kwame Ofori standing in a conference room at the World Bank LAND Conference in Washington DC](images/land-conference.jpg)

At the World Bank LAND Conference in Washington, D.C.

*The formal programme was one part of the trip. The conversations and wandering afterwards became part of it too.*

That is partly why this website is broader than my PhD. Research teaches you to narrow a question until it can be answered properly, and that discipline matters. But life keeps producing questions that do not yet fit inside a model, a journal article or a project, and I wanted somewhere to put those too. **EKO Perspectives** is that place. Some posts will be about agriculture or economics because that is where my research sits. Others will be about technology, science, Ghana, Africa, Ireland, a book, a place or a person. I do not expect them all to fit in one intellectual box. My own route has not.

## Still unfinished

Writing about a journey from the middle of it has an obvious danger: accidents start to look like plans. Decisions get more credit than they deserve because their consequences only became clear years later, and memory quietly rearranges the cast. I do not want this page to become a polished origin story in which every step led neatly to the next. Some things worked and some plans changed. Some opportunities arrived unexpectedly. I walked away from one PhD and began another, and places I expected to be temporary became important parts of my life.

I will come back to this page when memory supplies something I missed, when a new chapter changes the shape of the story, or when I realise I have not thanked somebody properly. For now, this is where I am.
