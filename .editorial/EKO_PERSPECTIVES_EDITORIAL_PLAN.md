# EKO Perspectives editorial plan

**Standing planning reference.** Use this alongside `EKO_EDITORIAL_WORKFLOW_v2.3.md` and `story-engine-library.md` when planning future EKO Perspectives posts.

The workflow remains canonical where this planning document is too rigid. In particular, story engines are a menu rather than a rotation rule, and the four-sentence test is useful for claim-testing and audit pieces but is not mandatory for every genre.

This pulls together the site review, the edits, and both rounds of reading into one plan, followed by twelve post ideas with why and how for each.

## 1. Where you stand

The foundations are good. The site is clean, sourced and ethically careful, and your research question, which farms and places carry the adjustment, is distinctive. The posts were readable but built from one template, cautious to the point of saying little, and written in a first person with almost none of you in them. The reading showed that strong land-use writing from 2008 to 2018 across Pearce, Searchinger, Fischer, Evans and farmdoc daily often does the opposite: it picks an engine suited to the story, names what it disagrees with, and reaches a verdict when the evidence supports one.

So the task is not more polish. It is more of you, more variety, and more conviction.

## 2. Principles for your voice

**Choose an engine before you draft.** Six useful families cover much of the material read:

- scene-led, where a real observed or documented place genuinely carries the story;
- claim-testing or audit;
- debate diagnosis;
- your own work explained;
- recurring data commentary;
- pattern assembly from public records.

Do not repeat a structure merely from habit. But do not force a different engine solely for novelty. The material decides.

**Use the four-sentence test when it fits:** what people think, what they missed, what the evidence finds, and so what. Searchinger's abstract is a strong model for claim-testing. If the story is narrative, historical, personal or profile-led, use an engine appropriate to that genre instead.

**Name the foil.** Strong pieces argue against or qualify something specific: a prior study, report, policy claim, institutional assumption or documented public argument. "Some people think" is not a useful foil.

**Look for the institution underneath.** A visible dispute may actually turn on ownership or tenure, accounting boundaries, baselines, incentives, spatial scale, implementation, displacement, leakage or another institutional mechanism. This should become one signature of EKO Perspectives when the evidence supports it.

**Make numbers mean something.** One well-chosen comparison can often do more than a paragraph of figures. Comparisons must be accurate and not sensational.

**Concede, then conclude.** Acknowledge real contrary evidence without letting caveats swallow the finding. State the claim once and firmly, and keep qualifications together rather than scattering hedges everywhere.

**Use the public record as named voices.** Without interviews, you can still use Dail debates, Teagasc reports, county development plans, court judgments, consultation submissions and other dated, attributable records. Never imply interview access or field observation that did not happen.

**Write about your own work, in its own time.** Each public paper may support several registers: the paper itself, a plain-language post, and a short note aimed at officials or practitioners. Do not disclose unpublished results or breach a co-author or journal embargo.

**Keep the craft and drop the copying.** Study how strong pieces are built, but never reuse another writer's sentences, distinctive phrases, invented scenes or unverifiable details.

## 4. Twelve post ideas

These are candidates, not assignments. Re-check the evidence, current policy status and site coverage before approving any topic. Ideas tied to unpublished papers wait until publication or use only public background material.

### 1. Ireland's tree-planting promise, year by year

**Engine:** promise-versus-performance audit.

**Why:** Afforestation targets and actual planting have diverged for years. This can connect to forestry and land-use interests without revealing unpublished work.

**How:** Take targets from successive national forest strategies and climate plans and planted area from official annual forest statistics. Build one chart of target against actual, then test explanations such as licensing, land prices, farmer participation and permanence of land-use change. End with the explanation or combination the evidence supports best. Verify every figure against the primary source.

### 2. One Irish number

**Engine:** recurring data commentary.

**Why:** Short posts can build discipline without lowering standards.

**How:** Use one scheduled public release and one figure or comparison. Possible sources include the Teagasc National Farm Survey, CSO agricultural prices and land statistics, EPA greenhouse-gas inventories and forest statistics. Ask one question per post and resist turning it into a miniature general essay.

### 3. The same acre: Leitrim and the competing claims on marginal land

**Engine:** place-led through public records, with a first-hand scene only if Elvis actually visits.

**Why:** Forestry, viable agriculture, rural livelihoods, biodiversity and national planting targets meet in one local argument.

**How:** Use Dail records, Leitrim forestry studies, county development-plan submissions and farm-press coverage. If visiting, describe only what was actually observed. Ask what a spatially explicit model can and cannot settle in a dispute like this.

### 4. Who owns the trees? Tenure under Ghana's cocoa farms

**Engine:** institution underneath the visible issue.

**Why:** This brings Ghana and Places & Development onto the site while fitting land-use and incentive questions.

**How:** Start from the law and how tenure rules shape incentives to retain or remove shade trees. Check the current legal position and reform status before writing. Use Forestry Commission documents, legislation and academic evidence. Use real personal memory only where genuinely relevant and clearly distinguished from sourced claims.

### 5. Why biofuel accounting kept changing its mind

**Engine:** debate diagnosis as intellectual history.

**Why:** This can explain the public background to corn-ethanol debates without using unpublished research results.

**How:** Follow the key papers and policy responses, explaining why estimates differed because of models, data, boundaries and counterfactuals. When the relevant EKO research becomes public, a behind-the-paper piece can link back to this background article.

### 6. The landowner isn't always the farmer

**Engine:** institution underneath, US edition.

**Why:** Voluntary land programmes may depend on owners as well as operators, particularly where land is rented.

**How:** Use USDA land-tenure and Census of Agriculture data. Pull exact shares from primary sources rather than memory. Explain what the ownership-operation split means for voluntary contracts without disclosing unpublished model results.

### 7. Hardin, Ostrom and the commonage

**Engine:** reading pair applied to a local institution.

**Why:** Ireland's western commonages provide a real case through which to examine famous competing ideas about shared resources.

**How:** Explain the two frameworks, then examine current treatment of commonage in Irish agri-environment schemes using official documents and stakeholder submissions. State which framework fits which part of the evidence rather than forcing an overall winner if the case is mixed.

### 8. Two futures for the uplands

**Engine:** two schools of thought.

**Why:** Rewilding, hill farming, grazing, biodiversity and rural livelihoods create a live empirical dispute.

**How:** Give each side its strongest credible evidence and supporters, identify genuine agreement, and end with the evidence or empirical question that matters most. Take a position only where the evidence earns one.

### 9. What China's Grain for Green can teach a voluntary scheme

**Engine:** comparative lesson from another country.

**Why:** The programme raises useful questions about participation, incentives and choice.

**How:** Explain the programme and evaluation evidence, then identify a narrow transferable lesson for Irish scheme design. Be explicit about differences between Chinese and Irish institutions so comparison does not become analogy by convenience.

### 10. Will Africa's yields grow fast enough?

**Engine:** your own work explained.

**Why:** This could become a flagship global research post when the underlying paper is public.

**How:** Publish only after the work is public and consistent with co-author and journal rules. Explain the research question, why persistence matters, the benchmark used and the public result in plain language with one figure.

### 11. Auditing a national promise: BECCS in Ireland

**Engine:** your own work explained through the audit genre.

**Why:** Feasibility audits can make national targets concrete.

**How:** After publication, explain the public findings. Before publication, a separate background post may examine the public dependencies of any national BECCS commitment, such as land, biomass supply, transport, storage and permanence, without using private results.

### 12. What I learned from talking to data

**Engine:** personal essay.

**Why:** The idea of learning when to stop talking and let the data answer back is one of the most distinctive personal lines on the site.

**How:** Use real episodes from Ghana, China, Galway or research collaboration only where Elvis wants them public. The point should be what those episodes taught about modelling, evidence, teaching or intellectual humility, not autobiography for its own sake.
