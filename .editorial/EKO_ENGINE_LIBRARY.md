# EKO Perspectives article-engine library

**Working editorial aid. Updated 1 October 2026.**

This file is a menu of possible article engines, not a house template. Choose the engine that fits the subject and evidence. Do not force variety for its own sake, but check recent EKO posts so a successful structure does not quietly become the default for every new piece.

The central rule is simple: **the form should follow what the article is actually trying to discover, test or explain.**

## Six useful engine families

### 1. Scene-led

Use when there is a real scene, place, event, object or first-hand observation that genuinely carries the argument.

Good for:
- place and development pieces;
- farm, conference or field visits;
- scientist profiles with documented scenes;
- historical pieces with a strong archival object or record.

Requirements:
- the scene must be witnessed, documented or responsibly sourced;
- never invent dialogue, atmosphere, chronology or observations;
- move from the scene into the governing question rather than treating the scene as decoration.

### 2. Claim test or audit

Start from a specific public promise, target, policy claim, model result or institutional assertion and test it against the record.

Good for:
- target versus performance pieces;
- policy implementation audits;
- feasibility checks;
- promises that can be compared with official data over time.

Useful structure:
1. state the claim precisely;
2. identify the evidence needed to test it;
3. show what the record says;
4. explain the mechanism behind any gap;
5. give a proportionate verdict.

The verdict should follow the evidence. Do not turn an audit into a ritual of caveats after the answer is already clear.

### 3. Debate diagnosis

Use when two or more schools of thought disagree and the useful contribution is explaining why.

Good for:
- contested environmental-policy questions;
- intellectual histories;
- disputes in which estimates differ because assumptions, counterfactuals, methods or definitions differ.

Tasks:
- identify the strongest version of each side;
- name the specific reports, studies, people or policy claims involved;
- isolate where the disagreement actually sits;
- distinguish empirical disagreement from value disagreement;
- say what evidence would change the answer.

Do not manufacture false balance. Unequal evidence should not be presented as equally strong merely because two positions exist.

### 4. Your own work explained

Use for public research that is already published or otherwise cleared for public discussion.

Good for:
- behind-the-paper posts;
- plain-language explanations of a model or method;
- short policy-facing notes derived from public research.

Focus on the decision problem before the machinery. Explain what the research was trying to learn, why existing evidence was insufficient, what the model or method added, and what the result does and does not support.

Do not publish confidential, unpublished, under-review or collaborator-restricted results. Coordinate with co-authors and journal or funder communication rules where relevant.

### 5. Data commentary

Build a short post around one number, release, chart or comparison.

Good for:
- scheduled CSO, EPA, Teagasc, forestry or farm-income releases;
- recurring "one number" posts;
- short evidence notes between longer features.

A strong data-commentary post usually does three things:
- explains what the number is;
- puts it against a useful comparator, baseline or distribution;
- shows what follows from the comparison.

One meaningful comparison is usually better than a paragraph of disconnected figures.

### 6. Pattern assembly from public records

Use when no single source contains the story and the contribution comes from joining several public records together.

Possible materials:
- Dáil or parliamentary debates;
- consultation submissions;
- court judgments;
- county or national plans;
- official reports and administrative records;
- public datasets;
- institutional statements.

This engine works best when the assembled record reveals an institution, incentive or implementation mechanism that is easy to miss when each document is read separately.

## The four-sentence planning test

Before drafting a substantial analytical piece, it can be useful to write four private sentences:

1. **What is the conventional claim or starting belief?**
2. **What does that framing miss?**
3. **What does the evidence in this article show?**
4. **Why does the difference matter?**

This is a diagnostic, not a visible article template. If the four sentences are weak, more research or a sharper angle may be needed. The published piece does not have to follow this order.

## Name the foil

When an article disagrees with something, identify the actual object of disagreement: a named policy, report, study, model assumption, official claim or school of thought.

Avoid vague constructions such as "some people think" unless the point genuinely is a diffuse public belief and that can be demonstrated.

## Look for the institution underneath

A recurring EKO strength should be asking what institutional rule or implementation arrangement produces the visible outcome.

Examples of questions:
- who owns or controls the resource?
- who signs the contract?
- who receives the payment?
- who bears the cost?
- what administrative boundary differs from the ecological or economic one?
- what incentive makes a seemingly irrational behaviour reasonable from the actor's position?

This is especially useful where national policy meets farms, households, landowners, firms or communities.

## Make numbers carry meaning

Prefer one or two well-chosen comparisons over a pile of figures. Useful comparators include:
- a policy target;
- a historical baseline;
- another region or enterprise;
- an area or population readers can picture;
- the distribution hidden by an average;
- the quantity one intervention would offset or undo.

Comparisons must remain accurate and not sensationalise the scale.

## Concede, then conclude

A fair article can acknowledge the strongest counterpoint and still reach a conclusion. Put important caveats where they matter, but do not scatter hedging through every paragraph.

A clear position is allowed when the evidence supports it. The strength of the language should match the strength of the evidence.

## Use the public record as voices

EKO Perspectives does not need invented scenes or anonymous pseudo-reporting. Where interviews are unavailable, public records can supply named, dated voices:
- parliamentary debates;
- consultation submissions;
- official testimony;
- speeches and public statements;
- court records;
- farm-organisation positions;
- institutional correspondence that is legitimately public.

Quote only when the exact wording matters; otherwise paraphrase accurately and link the source.

## Structural-variety check

Before drafting, inspect the most recent EKO pieces and ask:
- am I reusing the same kind of opening?
- am I repeating the same pivot phrases?
- am I ending in the same rhetorical move?
- am I using first person because it contributes evidence or judgment, or because the house style has trained me to?
- does another engine fit the subject better?

Back-to-back use of the same engine is allowed when it is genuinely the best form. The aim is not forced alternation. The aim is to prevent convenience from hardening into a formula.

## Craft without copying

Older journalism, research blogs and magazines can be studied for architecture, pacing, transitions, evidence placement and endings. Do not reuse another writer's sentences or imitate a living writer's distinctive voice. Reported-scene structures belong only where EKO has real reporting, archival evidence or first-hand observation to support them.
