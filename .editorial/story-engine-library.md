# Story engine library

This is a menu, not a template system. Choose an engine only after the evidence is understood. A piece may combine two engines if the material genuinely does so. Do not impose a rule that the same engine can never appear twice in a row; instead, use the site preflight to notice repetition and ask whether it is earned.

## Engine families

### 1. Scene or place led
Begin with a real place, person, event, document or physical object and widen into the larger question. Use only when the scene is first-hand or well documented. Never invent atmosphere to imitate reported journalism.

### 2. Contradiction or foil led
Start with something that appears true, desirable or settled, then introduce the fact that complicates it. The foil should be a real claim, policy assumption, study or widely held position, not a vague straw man.

### 3. Claim testing or policy audit
Structure around `what was promised or assumed -> what was measured -> where the claim holds -> where it does not -> implication`. Good for policies, targets, feasibility claims and anniversaries.

### 4. Debate diagnosis
Do not choose a side too early. Explain why a debate is stuck: different definitions, scales, baselines, objectives, metrics or evidence. This is useful when apparently opposed camps are answering different questions.

### 5. Behind the paper or behind the model
Explain how a research question arose, what decision or modelling problem became difficult, what was tried, what surprised you, and what the work can and cannot establish. This should contain something only the researcher could credibly tell.

### 6. One number or one comparison
Use a single comparison to make scale intelligible, then follow its implications. Do not overload the piece with unrelated numbers. A comparison can be financial, spatial, temporal or physical.

### 7. Pattern assembly from public records
Bring together parliamentary debates, plans, court judgments, agency documents, datasets or local submissions that are individually ordinary but collectively reveal a pattern. Attribute every voice and do not present desk research as field reporting.

### 8. Mechanism led
Name and explain the mechanism that connects events: displacement, rebound, leakage, lock-in, option value, tenure, accounting boundaries, spatial targeting, behavioural response or another mechanism supported by evidence. Prefer plain language before technical labels.

### 9. Timeline or policy-reversal story
Show how rules, incentives or outcomes changed over time. The movement should reveal causation, institutional learning or reversal, not just provide chronology.

### 10. People or places behind the science
Build around person + question + place + people + method + consequence. The laboratory, field station, institution, experimental plot or intellectual community can be part of the story. Avoid the lone-genius narrative and explain the central mechanism rather than calling work merely "groundbreaking."

## Choosing an engine

Before drafting, answer internally:

1. What is the most important thing this piece is trying to show?
2. What evidence actually carries that point?
3. What would a well-informed critic say?
4. Is the deeper issue really about land, or about measurement, ownership, accounting, incentives, spatial scale or implementation?
5. Which engine lets that evidence unfold with the least forcing?

If the engine cannot be named without distorting the evidence, do not name one. Write the piece naturally.

## Reading exemplars

Older E360 features, research blogs, academic op-eds, trade press, journal papers and classic essays are useful for studying architecture. There is no single E360 framework. Reported features, opinion columns, research blogs, interviews and journal articles use different engines.

When studying a reference piece:

- outline what the writer does, not how the sentences sound;
- read a strong rebuttal or competing interpretation when one exists;
- record what evidence carries the argument;
- note what would fail if the opening device or structure were copied onto a different subject;
- never reuse distinctive phrases or imitate one living writer's voice.

Some historical examples collected during EKO workflow development were only read through abstracts, excerpts or secondary accounts. Treat those as reading leads until the original has been reread. Do not encode an unverified summary as a permanent house rule.
